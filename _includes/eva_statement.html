A statement on Eva

Eva Yi Xie, a Princeton neuroscience PhD student, was detained by ICE on her way home from a neuroscience conference, where she’d just presented her research. She’s being held at the Caroline ICE facility in Virginia.

On Eva. Eva is a brilliant scientist. She has an astounding knowledge of ideas spanning neuroscience to AI; she has the remarkable ability to recall experiments from dozens and dozens of academic papers. Eva is a community builder, and a valued member of her community. She organizes events to bring together trainees and senior researchers, to bring together researchers in different fields. She is a valued reviewer for conferences in her field. As an undergraduate at MIT, she was a member of President Reif’s Presidential Advisory Cabinet, as well as the Chair of the MIT Judicial Review Board. Eva is a teacher. She has been a teaching assistant for classes from probability to deep learning to neuroscience. As a graduate student, she is involved in helping to set the curriculum of the Princeton Neuroscience Institute.

On Eva’s work. Eva’s research aims to understand how the brain makes decisions. The research is remarkable in its breadth: doing surgery on rats, training the rats to perform new tasks, analyzing the data, and building AI models that help explain the findings. Eva’s research is important because it helps us to understand the brain. As Eva put it: “If we don’t even understand the basic principles of these cognitive processes, do we truly understand ourselves?” Eva’s research is basic research, at the very fundamental level; it builds the foundation for medical innovations in the decades to come.

On her detention. I received a short message from Nathaniel and Carlos, Eva’s other co-advisors, on the afternoon of Nov 19th sharing the news: that Eva had been detained when flying back from the Society for Neuroscience conference. I was shocked and surprised. I’d heard about similar stories, but had never thought that it would happen at Princeton, and never had thought that it would happen to one of my students.

I was paralyzed for a few minutes. I then ran down to my lab and gathered lab members to share the news. One PhD student: “How can this be happening!?” Some members of the lab drove down to Virginia late that night. In the days since then, we have been working to secure legal representation, call lawmakers, and organize the community response.

One of the most amazing things has been seeing the number of people that have reached out to support Eva. Many heartwarming stories about how Eva has touched the lives of so many people in her community: undergraduate students for whom she was the star TA; college classmates with whom she worked on problem sets; PhD and postdocs with whom she ran experiments.

On effects of her detention. Eva’s detention has been frightening, especially to international students. These students are legally allowed to study and work in the US, but so was Eva. If Eva could be detained despite being legally allowed to be in the US, could this happen to other students that also were legally allowed to be in the US?

Some of my PhD students are nearing graduation, and thinking about whether they will remain in the US after graduation. Like Eva, these are some of the most brilliant young scientists in the world, who spend 5 years as doctoral students becoming the world’s expert in an area of science. Often this research is funded by the National Science Foundation, which recognizes the importance of the science and the importance of training the next generation of scientists. The message that Eva’s detention sends to these students is that they aren’t welcome in the US. The US needs the next generation of scientists, brilliant young researchers like Eva. We need scientists to help unlock breakthroughs in medicine, in biology, in AI.
